Baybayin is a pre-colonial Philippine writing system that flourished in the archipelago for centuries. It is an abugida, or syllabic alphabet, where each character represents a consonant-vowel syllable. Its elegant forms and logical structure offer a window into the linguistic and cultural landscape of the Philippines prior to Spanish colonization.

The Script

The traditional script is composed of seventeen unique characters. This set includes three primary vowels and fourteen consonants, each of which carries an inherent /a/ sound. This foundational structure is the key to understanding how the script is both efficient and expressive.

The Kudlit

The kudlit is the ingenious diacritical mark that modifies a consonant's vowel sound. By placing a dot or tick, the inherent /a/ can be changed to /e/-/i/ or /o/-/u/. This system eliminates the need for a much larger set of characters. Experiment with its effect on the character Ba (ᜊ) below.

Further Reading

Writing Rules & Orthography ▼

In traditional Baybayin, there was no mechanism for representing a final consonant in a syllable (a "coda"). For example, the word "bundok" (mountain) would be written as "bu-do," omitting the final 'n' and 'k' sounds. The reader was expected to use context to understand the intended word. Later, a special kudlit (the virama) was introduced by Spanish friars to cancel the vowel sound, but this was not part of the original script.

Historical Context & Revival ▼

The use of Baybayin waned during the Spanish colonial period as the Latin alphabet became the standard for education and administration. However, the script was never truly lost. In recent decades, there has been a powerful cultural revival, with artists, designers, and scholars championing its use as a symbol of Filipino identity and heritage. Today, it can be seen in modern art, tattoos, government seals, and even the Philippine passport.
